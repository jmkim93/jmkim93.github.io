Compute-First Optical Detection for Noise-Resilient Visual Perception

Jungmin Kim, Nanfang Yu & Zongfu Yu

ACS Photonics 12, 1137-1145 (2025)

Publisher pageRead PDF

A camera normally detects an image before software extracts useful features. This paper asks whether some of that processing should happen in light, before detector noise is added.

The order of operations changes the problem

In a detect-first pipeline, weak optical signals become noisy electronic measurements before a classifier sees them. The proposed system inserts a linear optical transformation ahead of the detector, so the sensor measures a representation designed for the recognition task.

That optical transformation does not need to form a conventional-looking image. It needs to preserve and redistribute the information that helps the downstream classifier make a decision.

Redistribution, not optical amplification

The field transformation P precedes intensity detection at channel α. In the discrete model, P is unitary: it conserves total optical energy while redistributing it among channels. A digital network maps the resulting noisy intensity vector to a prediction.

This distinction explains the noise benefit. Concentrating useful signal into selected channels can make it less vulnerable to intensity-independent dark noise. Photon noise still depends on the signal, so rearranging the light does not eliminate the statistics of photon detection. The paper treats both noise mechanisms explicitly.

Learn what the detector should receive

The optical transformation and digital classifier are optimized together for visual recognition. MNIST provides a controlled benchmark for examining how redistribution affects accuracy as detection noise increases. The study then considers an incoherent imaging implementation with designed phase masks, moving beyond the abstract matrix description.

The numerical results connect noise resilience to how the optical stage concentrates task-relevant signal. They suggest that the best measurement for a recognition task need not be the best conventional image.

The analysis focuses on photon and dark noise. Alignment errors, fabrication variation, and noise within a larger optical processor would require additional assessment before translating the result into a complete imaging system.
