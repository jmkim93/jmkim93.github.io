Photonic Systolic Array for All-Optical Matrix–Matrix Multiplication

Jungmin Kim, Qingyi Zhou, Zongfu Yu

Laser & Photonics Reviews 20, e01995 (2026)

Publisher pageRead PDF

When both matrices change, storing one of them as fixed optical weights becomes a limitation. This paper instead sends both operands through a photonic array as pulse trains and accumulates the answer locally.

A matrix product with two moving operands

Many photonic processors encode an input in light and keep a weight matrix in device settings. That arrangement is well suited to repeated multiplication by the same weights. Products between two changing inputs—such as those used in attention—motivate a different dataflow.

Here, columns of the two matrices become synchronized optical signals. In the paper’s indexing convention, each output cell computes

Cmn=∑kAkmBkn,C=ATBC_{mn}=\sum_k A_{km}B_{kn},\qquad C=A^{\mathsf T}BCmn​=k∑​Akm​Bkn​,C=ATB

The transpose reflects how the input vectors are arranged, rather than a different arithmetic operation. One output entry stays at each cell while successive pulses supply the terms in its inner product.

Interference supplies the product; detection supplies the sum

The two inputs enter a cell through perpendicular waveguides. A crossing and indexed branches distribute them through the array while tapping a portion toward a 50:50 beam splitter. The branch ratios and phases matter: each cell must receive the intended signal amplitudes and relative phase.

For real amplitudes a and b, with the specified phase relation, subtracting the two output intensities removes the individual intensity terms and leaves

ΔI=∣o1∣2−∣o2∣2=−2ab\Delta I=|o_1|^2-|o_2|^2=-2abΔI=∣o1​∣2−∣o2​∣2=−2ab

Photodetectors convert this difference into charge. Integrating over synchronized pulses adds the products in time, giving the desired inner product after calibration of the known sign and scale factors. Although the operands travel optically, accumulation and readout use photodetection.

Making the array compact enough to matter

Adjoint optimization designs the crossings, branches, and beam splitters for compactness, power distribution, and phase matching. Full-wave simulations test a 4 × 4 vector outer product and matrix multiplication in a 2 × 2 array, so the work examines operation beyond a single isolated multiplier.

Reported design estimate: 4.4 PMACs mm⁻² s⁻¹, calculated from simulated cell area and allowable pulse spacing.

This is a theoretical computing-density estimate for a numerical design. Practical throughput also depends on pulse generation and modulation, loss, and readout. The contribution is the architecture and its optical implementation strategy; a fabricated processor is not demonstrated.
