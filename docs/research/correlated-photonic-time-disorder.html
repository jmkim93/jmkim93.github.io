Research article

Unidirectional scattering with spatial homogeneity using correlated photonic time disorder

Jungmin Kim, Dayeong Lee, Sunkyu Yu & Namkyoo Park

Nature Physics 19, 726-732 (2023)

Publisher pageRead PDF

Can a medium control scattering direction without any spatial pattern? This paper shows how correlations in its time variation can provide that control.

Disorder along the time axis

Ordinary scattering engineering arranges material in space. Here the material remains spatially homogeneous, but its permittivity varies during a finite time interval. Spatial homogeneity preserves wavevector, while external modulation allows energy exchange with the optical field.

The crucial design variable is not simply how strongly the material fluctuates. It is how fluctuations at different times are correlated. Different temporal sequences can share the same statistics and therefore produce the same ensemble-averaged scattering behavior.

Two spectral regions control two scattering channels

Write the inverse permittivity as α(t) = αb[1 + Δα(t)]. The time structure factor S(Ω) is the Fourier transform of the autocorrelation of Δα. In the weak-scattering, long-modulation-time regime, the paper obtains

⟨PFW⟩≈ωb2T4S(0),⟨PBW⟩≈ωb2T4S(2ωb)\langle P_{\mathrm{FW}}\rangle\approx\frac{\omega_b^2T}{4}S(0),\qquad\langle P_{\mathrm{BW}}\rangle\approx\frac{\omega_b^2T}{4}S(2\omega_b)⟨PFW​⟩≈4ωb2​T​S(0),⟨PBW​⟩≈4ωb2​T​S(2ωb​)

Here T is the modulation duration and ωb is the background wave frequency. The powers refer to scattered components. Their dependence on different points of the same spectrum makes it possible to change one channel while suppressing the other.

This also gives a concrete inverse-design objective: shape a physically admissible correlation spectrum around Ω = 0 and Ω = 2ωb, then generate temporal realizations with those statistics. The spectrum must satisfy the constraints of a valid autocorrelation.

Direction is only one consequence

Numerical calculations test the predicted forward and backward scattering powers across ensembles of modulation sequences. The same framework links temporal order to scattering bandwidth, enabling designs for broadband scattering and resonance-free color filtering.

The result extends disorder engineering into time: useful optical responses can be encoded statistically without fabricating a spatial microstructure. The theory and simulations establish the mechanism; realizing it experimentally requires a material platform that can support the specified temporal modulation.
