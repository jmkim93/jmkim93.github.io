Quantum nonlinearity for optical neural computing

Qingyi Zhou, Jungmin Kim, Yutian Tao, Guoming Huang, Ming Zhou, Zewei Shao, Zongfu Yu

Nature Communications, article in press (2026)

Publisher pageRead PDF

Optical networks can mix signals efficiently. The harder step is making them nonlinear at useful light levels. This paper uses a single quantum emitter in a designed optical environment to supply that missing operation.

The bottleneck between linear layers

A sequence of linear optical transformations is still a linear transformation. Adding depth alone therefore does not give an optical neural network the nonlinear decision boundaries needed for tasks such as spiral classification. Conventional optical nonlinearities can provide an activation, but often demand high intensity to change the response appreciably.

The proposed alternative is a saturable quantum emitter. At weak excitation it scatters light strongly; as it saturates, that response changes. The design problem is to turn this microscopic effect into a useful and low-loss input–output relation between waveguides.

Design the environment around the emitter

The activation unit places a SiV⁻ color center inside an inverse-designed GaP-on-diamond structure. Adjoint optimization shapes the dielectric environment to strengthen light–matter interaction and control the interference between the directly transmitted and scattered fields. The emitter supplies the nonlinear physics; the surrounding structure makes it accessible through the optical ports.

Nonlinear frequency-domain simulations determine the unit’s amplitude transfer curve. That physical curve becomes the activation function in a trainable network. The trained linear transformations are then implemented as separately optimized photonic blocks, with emitter units inserted between them. This connects device design to learning without replacing the physical activation by an arbitrary digital function.

What “expressive power” means here

To compare different activation mechanisms, the paper follows a closed curve of input data through successive network layers. A curve that bends and folds more can represent more complicated feature-space geometry. Its total curvature is

Here s is arc length, T is the unit tangent, and κ is local curvature. The comparison uses the fitted growth of this geometric quantity with layer number ℓ:

Kℓ≈K0rℓ,log⁡Kℓ≈c+ℓlog⁡rK_\ell\approx K_0r^\ell,\qquad \log K_\ell\approx c+\ell\log rKℓ​≈K0​rℓ,logKℓ​≈c+ℓlogr

The factor r measures curvature growth per layer under a specified evaluation protocol. It is not classification accuracy, and it depends on input scale as well as the activation. The supplement evaluates a 15-layer, 512-neuron-wide digital network and fits the last five layers to establish the reference.

For an optical device, changing incident intensity changes the activation curve and therefore the fitted r(I). The proposed structure reaches r ≈ 1.14 at 0.24 nW/μm² in the main paper. The comparison threshold, r = 1.045, is the ELU digital baseline. The important link is from optical geometry to transfer curve, and from that curve to growth of representational complexity.

Supplementary Notes 10–11 give the fitting procedure and optical-intensity comparison.

From nonlinear classification to larger networks

Numerical demonstrations include spiral classification and reinforcement-learning tasks, where nonlinear optical networks outperform the linear baselines. These are tests of the complete learning pipeline, complementing the curvature-based comparison of the activation itself.

The paper also estimates how much optical power is required to drive nonlinear activations in larger models. Those estimates describe the contribution from nonlinearity, not the wall-plug power of an integrated computer. Emitter bandwidth, placement and spectral variations, optical losses, and hardware integration remain practical questions.
